    Frequency and period

    NSCOM03-02 Physical Communication Layer.pdfslides 6–7
    \[f = \frac{1}{T} \qquad T = \frac{1}{f}\]
    • f frequency in Hz (cycles per second)
    • T period in seconds (time for one cycle)
    Problem 1

    A sine wave completes 4 cycles in 1 s. What are its frequency and period?

    Show answer

    Answer

    \[\begin{aligned}f &= \frac{4\ \text{cycles}}{1\ \text{s}} = 4\ \text{Hz} \\ T &= \frac{1}{f} = \frac{1}{4} = 0.25\ \text{s}\end{aligned}\]
    Problem 2

    A signal has a period of 5 ms. What is its frequency in Hz? In kHz?

    Show answer

    Answer

    \[\begin{aligned}f = \frac{1}{T} = \frac{1}{5\times10^{-3}\ \text{s}} = 200\ \text{Hz} = 0.2\ \text{kHz}\end{aligned}\]

    Wavelength

    NSCOM03-02 Physical Communication Layer.pdfslides 9–10
    \[\lambda = \frac{c}{f}\]

    equivalently \(\lambda = c \cdot T\)

    • λ wavelength in metres
    • c propagation speed (3 × 10⁸ m/s in free space)
    • f frequency in Hz
    Problem 3

    Red light has a frequency of 4 × 10¹⁴ Hz and travels at 3 × 10⁸ m/s. What is its wavelength?

    Show answer

    Answer

    \[\begin{aligned}\lambda = \frac{3\times10^{8}}{4\times10^{14}} = 0.75\times10^{-6}\ \text{m} = 0.75\ \mu\text{m}\end{aligned}\]
    Problem 4

    A Wi-Fi radio transmits at 2.4 GHz through free space. What is the wavelength in centimetres?

    Show answer

    Answer

    \[\begin{aligned}\lambda = \frac{3\times10^{8}}{2.4\times10^{9}} = 0.125\ \text{m} = 12.5\ \text{cm}\end{aligned}\]

    Bandwidth (in Hertz)

    NSCOM03-02 Physical Communication Layer.pdfslide 13
    \[B = f_{\text{high}} - f_{\text{low}}\]
    • B range of frequencies in a composite signal or channel
    Problem 5

    A telephone line uses 300 Hz to 3300 Hz for data. What is its bandwidth?

    Show answer

    Answer

    \[\begin{aligned}B = 3300 - 300 = 3000\ \text{Hz}\end{aligned}\]
    Problem 6

    A composite signal has a bandwidth of 20 Hz and its highest frequency is 60 Hz. What is its lowest frequency? If it contains 5 equally spaced components, list them.

    Show answer

    Answer

    \[\begin{aligned}f_{\text{low}} &= f_{\text{high}} - B = 60 - 20 = 40\ \text{Hz} \\ \text{spacing} &= \frac{20}{4\ \text{gaps}} = 5\ \text{Hz} \;\Rightarrow\; 40,\ 45,\ 50,\ 55,\ 60\ \text{Hz}\end{aligned}\]

    Bits per signal level

    NSCOM03-02 Physical Communication Layer.pdfslide 14
    \[n = \log_2 L \quad\iff\quad L = 2^{n}\]
    • L number of signal levels
    • n bits carried by each level
    Problem 7

    A digital signal uses 8 voltage levels. How many bits does each level carry?

    Show answer

    Answer

    \[\begin{aligned}n = \log_2 8 = 3\ \text{bits per level}\end{aligned}\]
    Problem 8

    You want each signal level to carry 6 bits. How many levels are needed?

    Show answer

    Answer

    \[\begin{aligned}L = 2^{6} = 64\ \text{levels}\end{aligned}\]

    Bit duration and bit length

    NSCOM03-02 Physical Communication Layer.pdfslide 14
    \[t_{\text{bit}} = \frac{1}{R} \qquad \ell_{\text{bit}} = v \cdot t_{\text{bit}}\]
    • R bit rate in bps
    • v propagation speed of the medium

    The slide defines bit rate (bits sent in 1 s) and bit length (distance one bit occupies) in words; these are the formulas behind those definitions.

    Problem 9

    A link sends 1 Mbps over a cable where signals travel at 2 × 10⁸ m/s. How long is one bit in time and in metres?

    Show answer

    Answer

    \[\begin{aligned}t_{\text{bit}} &= \frac{1}{10^{6}} = 1\ \mu\text{s} \\ \ell_{\text{bit}} &= (2\times10^{8})(1\times10^{-6}) = 200\ \text{m}\end{aligned}\]
    Problem 10

    Same cable, but the rate goes up to 100 Mbps. What is the bit length now?

    Show answer

    Answer

    \[\begin{aligned}t_{\text{bit}} &= \frac{1}{10^{8}} = 10\ \text{ns} \\ \ell_{\text{bit}} &= (2\times10^{8})(10^{-8}) = 2\ \text{m}\end{aligned}\]

    Decibel (attenuation and gain)

    NSCOM03-02 Physical Communication Layer.pdfslides 20–22
    \[\text{dB} = 10\log_{10}\!\left(\frac{P_2}{P_1}\right)\]
    • P₁ power at point 1 (start)
    • P₂ power at point 2 (end)
    • ± negative = attenuated, positive = amplified; dB values along a path add up
    Problem 11

    A signal's power is reduced to one-half (P₂ = 0.5 P₁). What is the attenuation in dB?

    Show answer

    Answer

    \[\begin{aligned}\text{dB} = 10\log_{10}\frac{0.5\,P_1}{P_1} = 10\log_{10}0.5 = 10(-0.301) \approx -3\ \text{dB}\end{aligned}\]
    Problem 12

    An amplifier raises a signal from 2 mW to 200 mW. What is the gain in dB?

    Show answer

    Answer

    \[\begin{aligned}\text{dB} = 10\log_{10}\frac{200}{2} = 10\log_{10}100 = 10(2) = +20\ \text{dB}\end{aligned}\]

    Signal-to-noise ratio

    NSCOM03-02 Physical Communication Layer.pdfslides 25–26
    \[\text{SNR} = \frac{P_{\text{signal}}}{P_{\text{noise}}} \qquad \text{SNR}_{\text{dB}} = 10\log_{10}\text{SNR}\]

    reverse: \(\text{SNR} = 10^{\,\text{SNR}_{\text{dB}}/10}\)

    • SNR high = clean signal, low = noisy signal; convert both powers to the same unit first
    Problem 13

    Signal power is 10 mW and noise power is 1 µW. Find SNR and SNR_dB.

    Show answer

    Answer

    \[\begin{aligned}\text{SNR} &= \frac{10\times10^{-3}}{1\times10^{-6}} = 10{,}000 \\ \text{SNR}_{\text{dB}} &= 10\log_{10}10{,}000 = 10(4) = 40\ \text{dB}\end{aligned}\]
    Problem 14

    A channel is rated at SNR_dB = 30 dB. What is the plain (unitless) SNR? If noise power is 2 µW, what is the signal power?

    Show answer

    Answer

    \[\begin{aligned}\text{SNR} &= 10^{30/10} = 10^{3} = 1000 \\ P_{\text{signal}} &= 1000 \times 2\ \mu\text{W} = 2000\ \mu\text{W} = 2\ \text{mW}\end{aligned}\]

    Nyquist bit rate (noiseless)

    NSCOM03-02 Physical Communication Layer.pdfslides 29–30
    \[\text{BitRate} = 2B\log_2 L\]

    solve for levels: \(L = 2^{\,\text{BitRate}/2B}\)

    • B channel bandwidth in Hz
    • L number of signal levels; more levels = more bits, but the receiver must still tell them apart
    Problem 15

    A noiseless channel has a bandwidth of 3000 Hz and uses 2 signal levels. What is the maximum bit rate?

    Show answer

    Answer

    \[\begin{aligned}\text{BitRate} = 2 \times 3000 \times \log_2 2 = 2 \times 3000 \times 1 = 6000\ \text{bps}\end{aligned}\]
    Problem 16

    You need to send 48 kbps over a noiseless 4 kHz channel. How many signal levels are needed?

    Show answer

    Answer

    \[\begin{aligned}48{,}000 &= 2 \times 4000 \times \log_2 L \\ \log_2 L &= \frac{48{,}000}{8000} = 6 \\ L &= 2^{6} = 64\ \text{levels}\end{aligned}\]

    Shannon capacity (noisy)

    NSCOM03-02 Physical Communication Layer.pdfslides 31–32
    \[C = B\log_2\left(1 + \text{SNR}\right)\]
    • C capacity in bps (the upper limit)
    • B bandwidth in Hz
    • SNR plain ratio, not dB; convert dB first. Signal levels don't appear: they don't change capacity
    Problem 17

    A telephone line has a bandwidth of 3000 Hz and an SNR of 3162. What is its capacity?

    Show answer

    Answer

    \[\begin{aligned}C &= 3000\log_2(1 + 3162) = 3000\log_2 3163 \\ &= 3000 \times 11.62 \approx 34{,}860\ \text{bps}\end{aligned}\]

    This is the slide’s answer, which rounds log₂ 3163 to 11.62. Unrounded, log₂ 3163 = 11.627, giving about 34,881 bps.

    Problem 18

    A 2 MHz channel has an SNR of 255. What is its capacity? What would it be if SNR were 0?

    Show answer

    Answer

    \[\begin{aligned}C &= 2\times10^{6}\log_2(1 + 255) = 2\times10^{6} \times 8 = 16\ \text{Mbps} \\ \text{SNR} = 0 \;\Rightarrow\; C &= B\log_2 1 = 0\ \text{bps}\end{aligned}\]

    Nyquist and Shannon together

    NSCOM03-02 Physical Communication Layer.pdfslide 33
    \[C = B\log_2(1+\text{SNR}) \;\Rightarrow\; R \le C \;\Rightarrow\; L = 2^{R/2B}\]

    1. Shannon → upper limit C
    2. Pick a practical rate R below C
    3. Nyquist → R = 2B log₂ L, solve for L

    Problem 19

    A channel has a 1 MHz bandwidth and an SNR of 63. What are the appropriate bit rate and signal level?

    Show answer

    Answer

    \[\begin{aligned}C &= 10^{6}\log_2(1 + 63) = 10^{6} \times 6 = 6\ \text{Mbps (upper limit)} \\ \text{use } R = 4\ \text{Mbps:}\quad 4\times10^{6} &= 2\times10^{6}\log_2 L \\ \log_2 L &= 2 \;\Rightarrow\; L = 4\ \text{levels}\end{aligned}\]
    Problem 20

    A channel has a 500 kHz bandwidth and an SNR of 1023. Find the Shannon limit, then the number of levels needed if you run it at 4 Mbps.

    Show answer

    Answer

    \[\begin{aligned}C &= 500\times10^{3}\log_2 1024 = 500\times10^{3} \times 10 = 5\ \text{Mbps} \\ \text{use } R = 4\ \text{Mbps:}\quad 4\times10^{6} &= 2 \times 500\times10^{3}\log_2 L \\ \log_2 L &= 4 \;\Rightarrow\; L = 16\ \text{levels}\end{aligned}\]

    Propagation time

    NSCOM03-02 Physical Communication Layer.pdfslides 35–37
    \[T_{\text{prop}} = \frac{\text{Distance}}{\text{Propagation speed}}\]
    • d distance in metres
    • v propagation speed in m/s
    Problem 21

    Sender and receiver are 12,000 km apart and light travels at 2.4 × 10⁸ m/s. What is the propagation time?

    Show answer

    Answer

    \[\begin{aligned}T_{\text{prop}} = \frac{12{,}000 \times 1000}{2.4\times10^{8}} = 0.05\ \text{s} = 50\ \text{ms}\end{aligned}\]
    Problem 22

    A fibre link runs 2,000 km and signals travel at 2 × 10⁸ m/s. What is the propagation time? How far would the signal get in 3 ms?

    Show answer

    Answer

    \[\begin{aligned}T_{\text{prop}} &= \frac{2{,}000 \times 1000}{2\times10^{8}} = 0.01\ \text{s} = 10\ \text{ms} \\ d &= v\,T = (2\times10^{8})(3\times10^{-3}) = 600{,}000\ \text{m} = 600\ \text{km}\end{aligned}\]

    Transmission time

    NSCOM03-02 Physical Communication Layer.pdfslides 35–37
    \[T_{\text{trans}} = \frac{\text{Message size}}{\text{Bandwidth}}\]
    • size in bits (bytes × 8)
    • BW bandwidth in bps
    Problem 23

    A 2.5 KB email is sent over a 1 Gbps network. What is the transmission time?

    Show answer

    Answer

    \[\begin{aligned}T_{\text{trans}} = \frac{2500 \times 8}{10^{9}} = \frac{20{,}000}{10^{9}} = 0.00002\ \text{s} = 0.020\ \text{ms}\end{aligned}\]
    Problem 24

    A 5 MB image is sent over a 1 Mbps link. What is the transmission time?

    Show answer

    Answer

    \[\begin{aligned}T_{\text{trans}} = \frac{5{,}000{,}000 \times 8}{10^{6}} = \frac{40{,}000{,}000}{10^{6}} = 40\ \text{s}\end{aligned}\]

    Latency (total delay)

    NSCOM03-02 Physical Communication Layer.pdfslide 35
    \[\text{Latency} = T_{\text{prop}} + T_{\text{trans}} + T_{\text{queue}} + T_{\text{proc}}\]
    • Latency time from the first bit leaving the source until the whole message arrives
    • T < B throughput is always below bandwidth; jitter is the variation in delay between packets
    Problem 25

    A 1500-byte packet crosses 3,000 km at 2 × 10⁸ m/s on a 100 Mbps link. Queuing delay is 0.5 ms and processing delay is 0.2 ms. What is the latency?

    Show answer

    Answer

    \[\begin{aligned}T_{\text{prop}} &= \frac{3\times10^{6}}{2\times10^{8}} = 15\ \text{ms} \\ T_{\text{trans}} &= \frac{1500 \times 8}{10^{8}} = 0.12\ \text{ms} \\ \text{Latency} &= 15 + 0.12 + 0.5 + 0.2 = 15.82\ \text{ms}\end{aligned}\]
    Problem 26

    Take the 5 MB image above (40 s transmission) across the slide's 12,000 km path (50 ms propagation), with 2 ms queuing and 1 ms processing. What is the latency, and which term dominates?

    Show answer

    Answer

    \[\begin{aligned}\text{Latency} = 0.050 + 40 + 0.002 + 0.001 = 40.053\ \text{s}\end{aligned}\]

    Transmission time dominates: big message, slow link. For the 2.5 KB email on 1 Gbps, propagation dominates instead (50 ms vs 0.02 ms).

    Constants and units

    Signal (baud) rate

    NSCOM03-03 Physical Communication Layer - Digital Transmission.pdfslides 8–9
    \[S = c \times N \times \frac{1}{r}\ \text{baud}\]
    • S signal rate in baud (signal elements per second)
    • c case factor: worst 1, average ½, best 0
    • N data rate in bps
    • r data elements carried per signal element
    Problem 1

    A signal carries one data element per signal element (r = 1). The bit rate is 100 kbps. What is the average baud rate if c is between 0 and 1?

    Show answer

    Answer

    \[\begin{aligned}c &= \tfrac{1}{2}\ \text{(average)} \\ S &= \tfrac{1}{2} \times 100{,}000 \times \tfrac{1}{1} = 50{,}000\ \text{baud} = 50\ \text{kbaud}\end{aligned}\]
    Problem 2

    A scheme carries 2 data elements per signal element (r = 2) at 4 Mbps. What are the average and worst-case baud rates?

    Show answer

    Answer

    \[\begin{aligned}S_{\text{avg}} &= \tfrac{1}{2} \times 4\times10^{6} \times \tfrac{1}{2} = 1\ \text{Mbaud} \\ S_{\text{worst}} &= 1 \times 4\times10^{6} \times \tfrac{1}{2} = 2\ \text{Mbaud}\end{aligned}\]

    Minimum bandwidth of line codes

    NSCOM03-03 Physical Communication Layer - Digital Transmission.pdfslides 24–25, 30, 45
    \[B_{\min} = S\]

    NRZ-L / NRZ-I: \(S_{\text{avg}} = N/2\) · Manchester: 2 × NRZ \(= N\) · MLT-3: about \(N/4\)

    • Bmin minimum bandwidth in Hz, equal to the average signal rate
    • N data rate in bps
    Problem 3

    A system uses NRZ-I to transfer 1 Mbps. What are the average signal rate and the minimum bandwidth?

    Show answer

    Answer

    \[\begin{aligned}S &= c \times N \times \tfrac{1}{r} = \tfrac{1}{2} \times 10^{6} \times 1 = 500\ \text{kbaud} \\ B_{\min} &= S = 500\ \text{kHz}\end{aligned}\]
    Problem 4

    The same 10 Mbps stream is sent with NRZ-I, with Manchester, and with MLT-3. What minimum bandwidth does each need?

    Show answer

    Answer

    \[\begin{aligned}\text{NRZ-I:}\quad B_{\min} &= N/2 = 5\ \text{MHz} \\ \text{Manchester:}\quad B_{\min} &= 2 \times 5 = 10\ \text{MHz} \\ \text{MLT-3:}\quad B_{\min} &\approx N/4 = 2.5\ \text{MHz}\end{aligned}\]

    Manchester pays double the bandwidth for a mid-bit transition that keeps sender and receiver in sync.

    Clock drift

    NSCOM03-03 Physical Communication Layer - Digital Transmission.pdfslide 14
    \[N_{\text{rx}} = N \times (1 + \text{drift})\]

    extra bits per second \(= N \times \text{drift}\)

    • N sender data rate in bps
    • drift how much faster the receiver clock runs, as a fraction (0.1% = 0.001)
    Problem 5

    The receiver clock is 0.1% faster than the sender clock. How many bits per second does the receiver get at 1 kbps? At 1 Mbps?

    Show answer

    Answer

    \[\begin{aligned}1\ \text{kbps:}\quad N_{\text{rx}} &= 1000 \times 1.001 = 1001\ \text{bps (1 extra)} \\ 1\ \text{Mbps:}\quad N_{\text{rx}} &= 10^{6} \times 1.001 = 1{,}001{,}000\ \text{bps (1000 extra)}\end{aligned}\]
    Problem 6

    A receiver clock runs 0.2% fast on a 10 kbps link. How many extra bits per second does it read?

    Show answer

    Answer

    \[\begin{aligned}\text{extra} = 10{,}000 \times 0.002 = 20\ \text{bps} \;\Rightarrow\; N_{\text{rx}} = 10{,}020\ \text{bps}\end{aligned}\]

    Multilevel coding (mBnL)

    NSCOM03-03 Physical Communication Layer - Digital Transmission.pdfslides 34–37
    \[2^{m} \le L^{n}\]

    \(2^{m} = L^{n}\): exact mapping · \(2^{m} < L^{n}\): spare signals for noise immunity and error detection

    • m length of the binary data pattern
    • n length of the signal pattern
    • L number of levels: B = 2, T = 3, Q = 4
    Problem 7

    2B1Q sends 2 bits as 1 quaternary (4-level) signal element. Check that the mapping works.

    Show answer

    Answer

    \[\begin{aligned}2^{m} &= 2^{2} = 4 \text{ data patterns} \\ L^{n} &= 4^{1} = 4 \text{ signal patterns} \\ 4 &= 4 \;\Rightarrow\; \text{exact mapping}\end{aligned}\]
    Problem 8

    8B6T sends 8 bits as 6 ternary (3-level) signal elements. Is it valid, and how many signal patterns are left over?

    Show answer

    Answer

    \[\begin{aligned}2^{8} &= 256 \text{ data patterns} \\ 3^{6} &= 729 \text{ signal patterns} \\ 256 \le 729 \;&\Rightarrow\; 729 - 256 = 473 \text{ spare}\end{aligned}\]

    Block coding (mB/nB)

    NSCOM03-03 Physical Communication Layer - Digital Transmission.pdfslides 48–54
    \[N_{\text{new}} = N \times \frac{n}{m}\]

    extra codewords \(= 2^{n} - 2^{m}\)

    • m bits in each data group
    • n bits in the replacement code group (n > m)
    • N original data rate in bps
    Problem 9

    You need to send 1 Mbps using 4B/5B. What minimum bandwidth is needed with NRZ-I? With Manchester?

    Show answer

    Answer

    \[\begin{aligned}N_{\text{new}} &= 10^{6} \times \tfrac{5}{4} = 1.25\ \text{Mbps} \\ \text{NRZ-I:}\quad B_{\min} &= N/2 = 625\ \text{kHz} \\ \text{Manchester:}\quad B_{\min} &= N = 1.25\ \text{MHz}\end{aligned}\]

    NRZ-I needs less bandwidth but has a DC component problem; Manchester needs more bandwidth but has no DC component.

    Problem 10

    How many extra codewords does 4B/5B have? What rate does 8B/10B produce from a 1 Gbps stream?

    Show answer

    Answer

    \[\begin{aligned}2^{5} - 2^{4} &= 32 - 16 = 16 \text{ extra words} \\ N_{\text{new}} &= 10^{9} \times \tfrac{10}{8} = 1.25\ \text{Gbps}\end{aligned}\]

    Sampling rate (Nyquist)

    NSCOM03-03 Physical Communication Layer - Digital Transmission.pdfslides 64–71
    \[f_s = \frac{1}{T_s} \qquad f_s \ge 2 \times f_{\max}\]

    low-pass: \(f_{\max}\) = bandwidth · band-pass: \(f_{\max}\) unknown from bandwidth alone

    • fs sampling rate in samples per second
    • Ts sampling interval in seconds
    • fmax highest frequency in the signal
    Problem 11

    Telephone companies assume voice has a maximum frequency of 4000 Hz. What sampling rate do they use, and what is the sampling interval?

    Show answer

    Answer

    \[\begin{aligned}f_s &= 2 \times 4000 = 8000\ \text{samples/s} \\ T_s &= \tfrac{1}{8000} = 125\ \mu\text{s}\end{aligned}\]
    Problem 12

    A complex low-pass signal has a bandwidth of 200 kHz. What is its minimum sampling rate? What if it were a band-pass signal with the same bandwidth?

    Show answer

    Answer

    \[\begin{aligned}\text{low-pass:}\quad f_s &= 2 \times 200\ \text{kHz} = 400{,}000\ \text{samples/s}\end{aligned}\]

    For the band-pass signal it can’t be found: the bandwidth doesn’t say where the band starts or ends, so the maximum frequency is unknown.

    Quantization step and error

    NSCOM03-03 Physical Communication Layer - Digital Transmission.pdfslides 72–74, 77–78
    \[\Delta = \frac{\max - \min}{L}\]

    quantization error range: \(-\Delta/2\) to \(+\Delta/2\); each sample takes the midpoint of its zone

    • Δ height of each zone
    • L number of quantization levels (zones)
    Problem 13

    A signal ranges from −20 V to +20 V and uses L = 8 levels. Find the zone width and the midpoints.

    Show answer

    Answer

    \[\begin{aligned}\Delta &= \frac{20 - (-20)}{8} = 5\ \text{V} \\ \text{midpoints} &= -17.5,\, -12.5,\, -7.5,\, -2.5,\, 2.5,\, 7.5,\, 12.5,\, 17.5\end{aligned}\]
    Problem 14

    A 0 V to 10 V signal is quantized with L = 16. What is Δ, and what is the largest possible quantization error?

    Show answer

    Answer

    \[\begin{aligned}\Delta &= \frac{10 - 0}{16} = 0.625\ \text{V} \\ |\text{error}|_{\max} &= \frac{\Delta}{2} = 0.3125\ \text{V}\end{aligned}\]

    Bits per sample

    NSCOM03-03 Physical Communication Layer - Digital Transmission.pdfslide 75
    \[n_b = \log_2 L\]

    solve for levels: \(L = 2^{n_b}\)

    • nb bits per sample
    • L number of quantization levels
    Problem 15

    The −20 V to +20 V signal above uses L = 8. How many bits per sample, and what code does the −20 V to −15 V zone get?

    Show answer

    Answer

    \[\begin{aligned}n_b = \log_2 8 = 3 \text{ bits} \;\Rightarrow\; 000, 001, \ldots, 111\end{aligned}\]

    The lowest zone, −20 V to −15 V, is 000.

    Problem 16

    How many bits per sample does L = 256 need? How many levels does 5 bits per sample give?

    Show answer

    Answer

    \[\begin{aligned}n_b &= \log_2 256 = 8 \text{ bits} \\ L &= 2^{5} = 32 \text{ levels}\end{aligned}\]

    PCM bit rate

    NSCOM03-03 Physical Communication Layer - Digital Transmission.pdfslides 79–80
    \[\text{Bit rate} = n_b \times f_s\]
    • nb bits per sample
    • fs sampling rate (at least 2 × fmax)
    Problem 17

    We digitize the human voice (0 to 4000 Hz) with 8 bits per sample. What is the bit rate?

    Show answer

    Answer

    \[\begin{aligned}f_s &= 2 \times 4000 = 8000\ \text{samples/s} \\ \text{Bit rate} &= 8 \times 8000 = 64{,}000\ \text{bps} = 64\ \text{kbps}\end{aligned}\]
    Problem 18

    Audio up to 20 kHz is sampled at the Nyquist rate with 16 bits per sample. What is the bit rate?

    Show answer

    Answer

    \[\begin{aligned}f_s &= 2 \times 20{,}000 = 40{,}000\ \text{samples/s} \\ \text{Bit rate} &= 16 \times 40{,}000 = 640\ \text{kbps}\end{aligned}\]

    Bit rate and baud rate

    NSCOM03-04 Physical Communication Layer - Digital to Analog Transmission.pdfslides 5–7
    \[S = N \times \frac{1}{r} \qquad r = \log_2 L\]

    in analog transmission of digital data, \(S \le N\)

    • S baud rate (signal elements per second)
    • N bit rate in bps
    • r data bits per signal element
    • L number of different signal elements
    Problem 1

    An analog signal carries 4 bits per signal element, and 1000 signal elements are sent per second. Find the bit rate.

    Show answer

    Answer

    \[\begin{aligned}N = S \times r = 1000 \times 4 = 4000\ \text{bps}\end{aligned}\]
    Problem 2

    An analog signal has a bit rate of 8000 bps and a baud rate of 1000 baud. How many data elements does each signal element carry? How many signal elements are needed?

    Show answer

    Answer

    \[\begin{aligned}r &= \frac{N}{S} = \frac{8000}{1000} = 8 \text{ bits per element} \\ L &= 2^{r} = 2^{8} = 256 \text{ signal elements}\end{aligned}\]

    ASK bandwidth

    NSCOM03-04 Physical Communication Layer - Digital to Analog Transmission.pdfslides 9, 12–13
    \[B = (1 + d) \times S\]

    carrier \(f_c\) sits in the middle of the available band

    • B bandwidth in Hz
    • d modulation and filtering factor, between 0 and 1
    • S signal (baud) rate
    Problem 3

    The available bandwidth is 100 kHz, from 200 to 300 kHz. Using ASK with d = 1, what are the carrier frequency and the bit rate?

    Show answer

    Answer

    \[\begin{aligned}f_c &= \frac{200 + 300}{2} = 250\ \text{kHz} \\ S &= \frac{B}{1 + d} = \frac{100\ \text{kHz}}{2} = 50\ \text{kbaud} \\ N &= S \times r = 50 \times 1 = 50\ \text{kbps}\end{aligned}\]
    Problem 4

    The same 200–300 kHz band is used for full-duplex ASK with d = 1. What data rate does each direction get?

    Show answer

    Answer

    \[\begin{aligned}B_{\text{each}} &= \frac{100}{2} = 50\ \text{kHz} \\ S &= \frac{50}{1 + 1} = 25\ \text{kbaud} \;\Rightarrow\; N = 25\ \text{kbps each way}\end{aligned}\]

    Each direction gets its own carrier in the middle of its half of the band.

    FSK bandwidth

    NSCOM03-04 Physical Communication Layer - Digital to Analog Transmission.pdfslides 15–16
    \[B = (1 + d) \times S + 2\Delta f\]

    a 1 is \(f_1 = f_c + \Delta f\), a 0 is \(f_2 = f_c - \Delta f\)

    • 2Δf gap between the two carrier frequencies f1 and f2
    • d between 0 and 1
    • S signal (baud) rate
    Problem 5

    The available bandwidth is 100 kHz, from 200 to 300 kHz. Using FSK with d = 1 and 2Δf = 50 kHz, what are the carrier frequency and the bit rate?

    Show answer

    Answer

    \[\begin{aligned}f_c &= 250\ \text{kHz} \\ 100 &= (1 + 1)S + 50 \\ S &= \frac{100 - 50}{2} = 25\ \text{kbaud} \;\Rightarrow\; N = 25\ \text{kbps}\end{aligned}\]
    Problem 6

    Binary FSK sends 10 kbps with d = 0.5, and the two carriers are 30 kHz apart. What bandwidth is needed?

    Show answer

    Answer

    \[\begin{aligned}S &= N = 10\ \text{kbaud} \quad (r = 1) \\ B &= (1 + 0.5)(10) + 30 = 45\ \text{kHz}\end{aligned}\]

    Multilevel FSK bandwidth

    NSCOM03-04 Physical Communication Layer - Digital to Analog Transmission.pdfslides 18–19
    \[B = (1 + d) \times S + (L - 1)\,2\Delta f = L \times S\]

    with \(d = 0\) and carriers spaced \(2\Delta f = S\) apart

    • L number of frequencies, \(2^{r}\)
    • S signal (baud) rate
    Problem 7

    Data is sent 3 bits at a time at 3 Mbps on a 10 MHz carrier. Find the number of frequencies, the baud rate, and the bandwidth.

    Show answer

    Answer

    \[\begin{aligned}L &= 2^{3} = 8 \\ S &= \frac{3\ \text{Mbps}}{3} = 1\ \text{Mbaud} \;\Rightarrow\; 2\Delta f = 1\ \text{MHz} \\ B &= 8 \times 1\ \text{M} = 8\ \text{MHz}\end{aligned}\]
    Problem 8

    A 4-FSK system sends 2 Mbps with d = 0 and its carriers spaced one baud apart (2Δf = S). What are the baud rate and the bandwidth?

    Show answer

    Answer

    \[\begin{aligned}r &= \log_2 4 = 2 \;\Rightarrow\; S = \frac{2\ \text{Mbps}}{2} = 1\ \text{Mbaud} \\ B &= 4 \times 1\ \text{M} = 4\ \text{MHz}\end{aligned}\]

    PSK and QPSK bandwidth

    NSCOM03-04 Physical Communication Layer - Digital to Analog Transmission.pdfslides 21, 24, 26
    \[B = (1 + d) \times S\]

    BPSK: \(r = 1\) · QPSK: \(r = 2\), \(L = 4\)

    • S signal (baud) rate, \(N/r\)
    • d between 0 and 1
    Problem 9

    Find the bandwidth for a signal transmitting at 12 Mbps with QPSK, d = 0.

    Show answer

    Answer

    \[\begin{aligned}S &= N \times \tfrac{1}{r} = \frac{12\ \text{Mbps}}{2} = 6\ \text{Mbaud} \\ B &= (1 + 0) \times 6 = 6\ \text{MHz}\end{aligned}\]
    Problem 10

    The same 12 Mbps is sent with BPSK, also with d = 0. What bandwidth is needed?

    Show answer

    Answer

    \[\begin{aligned}S &= \frac{12\ \text{Mbps}}{1} = 12\ \text{Mbaud} \\ B &= (1 + 0) \times 12 = 12\ \text{MHz}\end{aligned}\]

    QPSK carries 2 bits per signal element, so it halves the baud rate and needs half the bandwidth of BPSK (6 MHz vs 12 MHz).
